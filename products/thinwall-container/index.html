What thinwall moulding demands from the tool

Thinwall containers are among the harder parts to mould well. The wall is thin, so the melt has to fill the cavity before it freezes off, and that means high injection speed and high pressure. That same pressure pushes against the core and tries to shift it, so the plates have to be stiff and the core properly supported. If it moves, the wall comes out thick on one side and thin on the other, and the container stacks badly or splits at the rim.

The cycle on a thinwall part is dominated by cooling, not by filling, so the cooling layout decides how many parts an hour the tool will give you. Ejection has to lift a thin, still-warm part without denting or stretching it. And in a multi-cavity tool every cavity has to fill at the same instant, which is why gate position and runner balance matter as much as the cavity itself.

Hot runner or cold runner

We build thinwall tools both ways. A hot runner takes the sprue and runner waste out of every shot, keeps the gate clean on a food container, and removes the regrind step -- on a high-volume container that is usually what pays for the tooling. Our 500 ml square food container mould (FC-112) is a hot runner tool. A cold runner stays simpler to run and maintain, and for shorter runs it is often the sensible choice. We will tell you which one your part and your volume justify rather than selling you the more expensive answer by default.

Food contact surfaces

Containers and cutlery that hold food need a cavity surface that releases cleanly and does not trap residue. We polish for food-grade resin and build the tool so parts come away cleanly shot after shot.

Mould steels we use for thinwall work

Thinwall tools run fast and run long, so the steel has to hold its surface and resist the clamping and injection loads. We work in P-20, 1.2311 and 1.2738 for cavity and core blocks, and H-13 and D-2 where a harder, more wear-resistant surface is needed.
